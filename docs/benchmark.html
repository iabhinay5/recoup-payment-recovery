The problem

Five problems, one remedy

Razorpay documents 26 distinct reasons a payment can fail. A conventional dunning system treats them as one bucket and retries on a fixed schedule — day 1, day 3, day 5, day 7. That is wrong in both directions at once.

An card_expired decline retried four times cannot succeed; the card is gone. A bank_technical_error retried an hour later hits a bank that is still down, burning a capped attempt and adding load to a failing rail. And a payment_cancelled decline has a silent-retry success probability of exactly zero — the customer walked away, and only reaching them brings them back.

Only insufficient_funds is genuinely a timing problem. It is 42% of failures, and it is the one place where when you retry is the whole question.

Read the third row first

Aggressive retry recovers 87.6% of revenue — nominally the best number on the board, and 2.0 points above ours. It gets there by recovering 9.4 points fewer payments: 59.7% against our 69.1%. The gap is the heavy tail in payment amounts, where a handful of large recoveries move the revenue column more than many small ones. It also spends 1.65 attempts on every payment, including the ones that can never succeed, against our 1.26. Attempts are not free: they consume issuer goodwill, rail capacity and customer patience, none of which appear in a recovery-rate column.

The first row of the table is the thesis

Remove session-awareness and the policy scores 57.8% — below the 58.1% baseline it exists to beat. Nearly the entire advantage comes from one idea: some declines need the customer back, and no retry schedule can substitute for that. Not from clever timing, and not from the model.

The third row is the one worth defending out loud. Razorpay's published resolution for card_declined is to advise the customer to try another card, not to silently retry the one that failed. Following that costs 0.22 points. Correctness here is close to free, which is what makes it a decision rather than a trade-off.

The worst case sits at the pessimistic end of the parameter that most directly drives the mechanism, and it still returns +6.2 points. That is the claim worth making: not “+10.7 under our assumptions” but at least +6.2 under any plausible one.

Two of the 45 configurations fall outside the calibration gate, both at the widest settings of the one parameter that was fitted rather than sourced. They are reported rather than dropped, and nothing here rests on them.

Recovery is modelled through mechanisms, not through a fitted curve. Insufficient funds clear because a balance recovers on a salary cycle; bank errors clear because an outage ends; cancelled payments clear only when outreach brings someone back. The 58% figure is therefore a check on those mechanisms rather than an input to them — a fitted curve would have handed the answer straight to the policy being tested.

Evidence

The salary cycle is real, and it is public

The balance mechanism assumes Indian consumers have more capacity just after a month-start credit. That was an assumption until NPCI's daily statistics were checked against it. It validates a simulator mechanism — it is not where the recovery comes from, and the ablations above say so plainly.

Evidence

Banks fail differently, by a factor of six

The denominator trap

Pooled across all 741 UPI member banks, outages occur at 0.00121 per bank-day. But that denominator is dominated by hundreds of tiny cooperative banks processing almost nothing. State Bank of India's own rate is 0.0988 per day — 82× higher. A merchant never meets the average bank; it meets the banks its customers actually use. Taking the pooled figure at face value would have understated rail risk by two orders of magnitude while looking more rigorous.

Provenance

Which numbers are real

A simulated result is worth exactly as much as its willingness to say where its inputs came from. scripts/audit_parameters.py prints this ledger and exits non-zero if any invented parameter escapes the sensitivity sweep. A second check runs beside it: every swept parameter must measurably move the outcome. Two did not — one was read by no code at all — and sweeping them had been proving nothing while the audit reported everything accounted for.

Limitations

What this does not show

The magnitude depends on parameters nobody publishes

The +10.7 point figure rests on parameters with no public source, chiefly the outreach response rate. The sweep bounds that: across 45 configurations the uplift ranges from +6.2 to +15.2 points. Read +6.2 as the defensible floor and +10.7 as the central estimate, not as a precise quantity.

The direction is safe, and for a specific reason

Retrying a payment_cancelled decline has probability zero — that comes from Razorpay's documentation, not from any parameter we chose. Any outreach response rate above zero makes outreach the better action for that class. The sign is guaranteed by the mechanism; only the size is uncertain.

The salary cycle is not where the win comes from

The bandit converged on retrying at +6h — the earliest permitted — for 68% of decisions, and a payday-targeting policy tested worse than the baseline. With the calibrated shortfall, most balances recover within hours. The win is routing, not salary-cycle timing. The more appealing story is the wrong one.

Revenue figures carry a heavy tail

Simulated failed-payment amounts have a mean of ₹31,009 against a median of ₹3,479. Recovery rate by count is robust to this; revenue-weighted figures are not, and should be read with that in mind.

No live-mode transactions, ever

Razorpay test mode only. Recoup schedules payment retries, so a live key would move real money — the credential check refuses anything that is not rzp_test_.
